# Part One — Why you can't see it

---

## 1. The horoscope problem

Read this and see whether it sounds like you.

> You have a great need for other people to like and admire you, yet you tend
> to be critical of yourself. While you have some personality weaknesses, you
> are generally able to compensate for them. You have considerable unused
> capacity that you have not turned to your advantage. At times you have
> serious doubts as to whether you have made the right decision.

Most people, reading that, feel a small click of recognition. In 1948 a
psychologist named Bertram Forer handed a version of it to his students as
their individual personality results, having assembled it from a newsstand
astrology column. He asked them to rate its accuracy out of five. The average
was 4.26.

The sentences work because they are true of nearly everyone while sounding as
though they were written about one person. This is the trap every strengths
tool falls into, and it is worth understanding precisely, because avoiding it
is the whole design problem.

### Why "you are an Achiever" tells you nothing

Suppose a test tells you that you are high in conscientiousness. Set aside
whether that's accurate. Ask instead: what can you *do* with it?

You can't write it on an application, because everyone writes it. You can't
say it in an interview, because it's the answer they've heard nine times that
morning. You can't choose between two jobs with it, because both jobs would
prefer a conscientious person. You can't decide what to delegate with it,
because it doesn't say what specifically degrades when someone else does it.

The information is real, and it is not *usable*, because it doesn't
distinguish. A trait tells you where you sit on a spectrum that every human
being sits on somewhere. And things that everyone has some amount of are not
what anyone is chosen for.

### What people are actually chosen for

Think about the last time you recommended someone. Not politely — genuinely,
because you wanted the thing to go well.

You almost certainly didn't say "she's conscientious." You said something more
like: *she'll work out what's actually going on*, or *if he's on it, it gets
done*, or *she'll make it make sense to everyone else in the room*. You named
a specific, repeatable way of being useful. You named a function, not a trait.

And here's the thing worth noticing: you produced that sentence instantly,
without effort, about somebody else. You know exactly what your friends are
for. It takes no work at all.

Now try it about yourself. Most people stall.

### The shape of the answer

This book proposes that what you're looking for is not a trait but an
**overlap** — four things that have to be true at once:

- something that **charges** you rather than draining you;
- something that is **easy** for you and hard for other people;
- something other people **already come to you for**;
- something that sits at an **unusual combination**, rather than in one skill.

Any one of those on its own is a dead end. Something you love and are bad at
is a hobby. Something you're good at and hate is a liability with a payslip
attached. Something you're good at that nobody needs is a private pleasure.
And something valuable that a thousand other people also do is a commodity,
priced accordingly.

Where all four overlap is a small, specific, oddly-shaped territory. That's
the thing. The rest of Part One is about why you can't see yours.

## 2. Suspiciously easy

Here is the central difficulty, and it's a genuinely strange one.

**Your biggest advantage is invisible to you precisely because it is an
advantage.** The easier something is for you, the less it registers as a skill,
because you have no experience of it being hard. You assume everyone can do it.
You assume it doesn't count.

Ask someone who reads a room effortlessly how they do it and they'll look
puzzled and say something like "you just… look at people?" They are not being
modest. They genuinely cannot see the thing they are doing, because from the
inside it feels like nothing at all. It feels like noticing that it's raining.

Meanwhile the same person will happily list, at length, the things they find
difficult and have had to work at. Those feel like real skills, because effort
was involved. So they build an identity around their second-best qualities and
give away their first-best for free, often without noticing they've given
anything.

### The tax on being good at something

There's a cost that comes with this, and most people have paid it without
naming it.

If a thing is easy for you, you tend to be slightly contemptuous of it. You
undercharge for it, literally or figuratively. You do it as a favour. You throw
it in. When someone thanks you for it you feel a bit fraudulent, because it
cost you nothing, and it seems wrong to accept credit for something that wasn't
hard.

You may also be quietly irritated when other people can't do it — which is one
of the most reliable tells there is. Impatience is diagnostic. If you find
yourself thinking *why is this taking them so long, it's obvious*, you have
just located an asymmetry. The thing is not obvious. It's obvious **to you**,
and that difference is exactly where your value lives.

### Three ways to catch it

Because you can't observe this directly, you have to come at it sideways. Three
angles that work:

**The complaint.** What have people criticised about you more than once? *You
overthink it. You just went ahead and did it. You take it too personally.* A
recurring criticism is almost always a strength with the volume turned up too
far, and it points at the strength more reliably than a compliment does,
because people are more precise when they're annoyed.

**The drift.** What do you catch yourself doing when you're meant to be doing
something else? Not what you'd list as a hobby — what your attention slides
towards on its own when you stop steering it. Reorganising something so it
makes more sense. Reading past the point of usefulness. Messaging someone who'd
find this interesting. Attention is a better witness than intention.

**The group project going badly.** When a shared thing starts to fail and
nobody is saying so, people fall into roles without discussing it. One person
quietly rebuilds it. One names the problem out loud. One gets everybody moving.
One holds the people together. Which one do you become? You don't decide this.
It happens to you, which is what makes it good evidence.

## 3. What you'd do for free, badly, forever

Ease on its own isn't enough. There is a second condition, and skipping it is
how people end up successful and miserable.

**A strength you resent is not an asset. It's a liability that pays well.**

Most of us have at least one. Something you're demonstrably good at, that
people reliably ask you for, and that costs you something every single time.
You can do it. You are known for it. And after two hours of it you need to lie
down in a dark room.

That thing is not your secret sauce, however good you are at it. Build a life
on it and you'll build a life you have to recover from. The reason is
arithmetic rather than romance: an ability that drains you can only be spent in
short bursts, and it will never compound, because you'll avoid the situations
that would develop it further.

### The Saturday test

So the second question is about **energy**, and the cleanest way to ask it is
to remove obligation entirely.

Two free Saturdays, same weather, nothing you have to do. One you spend deep in
a single thing on your own, hours vanishing without your noticing. The other
you spend out among people, plans forming, leaving with three new things
happening. Which one leaves you *charged* rather than flat?

There's no correct answer, and that matters — the moment one option is more
admirable than the other, you'll pick the admirable one and learn nothing about
yourself. Both of those Saturdays are good Saturdays. That's what makes the
question work.

A sharper version: **what would you do for free, badly, forever?** Not what
you're best at. Not what you'd want to be seen doing. The thing you'd keep
doing anyway, at a mediocre standard, with nobody watching, because the doing
of it is the point.

- Take something messy and make it make sense.
- Take something rough and make it good.
- Take something stuck and get it moving.
- Take something dull and make it land.

One of those is more *you* than the others. Notice which, and notice that you
knew immediately.

### The good kind of tired

One more angle, useful because it uses memory rather than prediction — and
people are far better witnesses to what they've done than forecasters of what
they'd enjoy.

At the end of a long, hard day, there is a kind of tired that feels *earned*
and a kind that feels merely spent. Think of a day of the first kind. What had
you actually been doing?

You worked something out. You made something. You carried people through
something. You brought someone round to something.

Whichever it was, that's the shape of work that pays you back rather than
merely paying you.

## 4. The evidence you didn't ask for

Everything so far has relied on your own account of yourself, which is a
problem, because you are not a reliable narrator on this subject. Not because
you're dishonest — because you're inside.

So the third condition is the one you can't argue with: **what do other people
actually come to you for?**

Not what you wish they came for. Not what you'd be best at if anyone ever
asked. What they turn up asking for, repeatedly, without being prompted.

### Why other people see it better

In the early 2000s a group of researchers at Michigan developed something
called the Reflected Best Self Exercise. The method is disarmingly simple: you
ask a set of people who know you — colleagues, friends, family, deliberately
from different parts of your life — to write you a short story about a time
they saw you at your best. Then you read the stories together and look for what
repeats.

What tends to happen is that people find the same thing named again and again
by people who have never met each other. And it's very often not the thing the
subject would have led with.

This works because other people have information you structurally cannot have.
They have seen you from outside. They've watched you do the effortless thing
and register it as remarkable, which you didn't, because it felt like nothing.
They have also, crucially, *chosen* to come to you rather than someone else —
and a choice is a much harder piece of evidence than an opinion.

### Asking it of yourself, honestly

Most people won't run a full feedback exercise, so the practical substitute is
to interrogate your own memory for evidence that already exists. Three
questions that work:

**What do people come to you for?** Not abstractly — think of actual occasions.
A second opinion they'll actually trust. Someone to make it happen. It
explained in plain words. An introduction. Someone to spot what's missing. It
done properly rather than just done. A fresh idea when everything's stale.
Someone to calm it down.

**When someone genuinely thanks you, what for?** Seeing something they'd
missed. Doing something they couldn't. Saying something they needed to hear.
Being there when it mattered. Opening a door.

**The last time someone asked for your help specifically** — you, not just
anyone available — what was it about? This one is worth the extra effort of
recalling a real occasion rather than summarising an impression. Specific
memories are much harder to flatter than general ones. Your summary of yourself
is a story you've edited. A particular Tuesday is not.

### Finish the sentence

Then there is the version that cuts through all of it, and it's worth writing
down before you read on:

> People come to me when ______________.

Most people, given ten seconds and no chance to polish, produce something
startlingly specific. *When something's gone quiet and nobody wants to say why.
When they've been staring at it too long. When they need someone to actually
decide.*

That sentence is usually closer to the truth than anything you'd write on a CV,
because you didn't have time to make it respectable.

## 5. The overlap, not the skill

The last condition is the one that turns *good* into *valuable*, and it's the
one most people get wrong, because they've been taught to think about it the
other way round.

The instinct is to specialise: find your thing, get better at it than everyone
else, be the best. It's a clean story and it works for a small number of
people, most of whom started at seven with a violin.

For everyone else there's a better route, and it's arithmetic.

### The maths of being rare

Suppose you're good at something — genuinely, top ten percent. That's one in
ten. There are a lot of people in a ten percent slice, and if that's your whole
proposition you're competing with all of them.

Now suppose you're top ten percent at *two* things that don't usually go
together. Understanding a system and explaining it. Working with numbers and
with stories. Making things and reading people. If those two are reasonably
independent, you're now roughly one in a hundred.

Add a third and you're one in a thousand, without ever having been the best in
the room at anything.

This is not a trick. It's the reason the person who can do the technical work
*and* sit in front of a client gets paid more than either specialist — not
because they're better at either half, but because the combination is scarce
and the coordination cost of hiring two people is real.

### Where the money actually is

The useful consequence: **value does not live in the skill. It lives in the
overlap.** And overlaps are much easier to build deliberately than excellence
is, because you already have several — you've just never counted them as an
asset, because each one on its own is unremarkable.

Some combinations that recur:

- **Building things and understanding people.** You sit between the thing
  being made and the people it's for. Both sides find you unusually easy to
  work with, and neither can quite say why.
- **Numbers and stories.** You move between the evidence and the narrative
  without dropping either. Rare, because most people are trained into one and
  faintly suspicious of the other.
- **Inside the system and outside it.** You know how it really works, which
  gives you standing, and you're not captured by it, which gives you sight.
- **Real depth and a beginner's curiosity.** You know a great deal and still
  ask the naive question, which is where most breakthroughs come from.
- **The whole picture and the tiny details.** Most people hold one at the cost
  of the other.
- **Getting it done and keeping people whole.** Extremely rare under pressure,
  which is exactly when it's worth most.

### The question to ask yourself

Not *what am I best at?* — that's the specialist's question and you'll probably
answer it wrongly and demoralise yourself in the process.

Ask instead: **which two worlds do I have a foot in that most people around me
don't?**

That's your edge. It's the reason someone would choose you over a person with a
better CV, and it's almost always the thing you've been treating as a
distraction from your real work rather than as the work itself.
