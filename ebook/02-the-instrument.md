# Part Two — The instrument

---

## 6. The four E's

Part One made an argument in prose. This chapter turns it into something you
can actually run.

Four conditions, each one a question:

| | The question | What it protects against |
|---|---|---|
| **Energy** | What charges you rather than draining you? | Building on a strength you resent |
| **Ease** | What is suspiciously easy for you? | Missing your own advantage because it feels like nothing |
| **Evidence** | What do people actually come to you for? | Believing your own press |
| **Edge** | What unusual combination do you carry? | Competing as a commodity |

And two relationships worth holding onto:

> **Unique** = Energy × Edge.
> **Valuable** = Ease × Evidence.
> **Secret sauce** = all four.

That multiplication sign is doing real work. These aren't points to accumulate;
if any one of them is near zero, the product is near zero. A rare combination
that nobody needs is a curiosity. A needed skill that flattens you is a job
you'll leave.

### Why the order matters

The four are deliberately sequenced, and it isn't arbitrary.

**Energy first**, because it's the easiest to answer honestly. Nobody has a
strong incentive to lie about which Saturday they'd enjoy, so it warms you up
on questions with low stakes and gets you used to answering as yourself.

**Ease second**, once you've stopped performing. By now you're describing
behaviour rather than aspiration, which is when the "isn't this obvious to
everyone?" answers start surfacing.

**Evidence third**, because it's the corrective. Everything before it was
self-report. This is the point where the instrument stops asking what you
think and starts asking what happened.

**Edge last**, because it needs the other three. You can't identify a useful
combination until you know what the components are.

### What gets weighted

Not all four count equally, and it's worth knowing why before you answer.

Evidence carries the most weight, because it's the hardest channel to flatter.
Ease is next — an asymmetry you can feel is better evidence than a preference
you can state. Energy and Edge count for less, not because they matter less to
your life, but because they're the most vulnerable to wishful thinking.

If that seems harsh, consider the alternative. An instrument that weights your
self-image most heavily will return your self-image, elegantly formatted.

## 7. The eight signals

Underneath the four questions sit eight ways of being useful. Every answer you
give pushes weight onto some of them.

They are not types. You have all eight, in some proportion, and the proportion
is the point. Read them and you'll probably feel two or three light up — that
recognition is doing real diagnostic work, so pay attention to it.

### The eight

**Pattern** — *You see the structure underneath.* You notice how things relate.
Where other people experience a pile of separate problems, you experience one
problem with a shape. You connect dots other people have to be shown.

**Insight** — *You go to the bottom of things.* Surface answers don't satisfy
you. You ask the extra two questions, past the point of comfort, until the
answer stops moving. You're often quiet early and then say the thing that
reorganises the conversation.

**Craft** — *You make it concrete, and then you make it good.* You close the
distance between an idea and a real object, which is where most people quietly
give up. Things you touch tend to still be standing years later.

**Drive** — *You start before it's comfortable and finish after it stops being
fun.* You have a low tolerance for stalling. The last ten percent, where most
efforts die, is where you're at your best.

**People** — *You read the room accurately and move it deliberately.* You carry
an unusually good map of who's good at what, who's struggling, who'd get on
with whom. You act on it rather than just noticing.

**Signal** — *You make things land.* Complicated goes in, obvious comes out.
You find the sentence, the analogy, the example that makes a hard idea click.
People leave conversations with you feeling cleverer.

**Care** — *You hold things together.* You notice what would have gone wrong
and handle it before it does, usually without mentioning it. The clearest
evidence of your work is a long run of nothing going wrong.

**Play** — *You find the third option.* When a room has narrowed to two bad
choices you're the one who says "or…". You're constitutionally unable to accept
that the current way is the only way.

### A note on the ones you don't have

Your two or three weakest signals are not deficiencies to fix. They are the
price of the ones you have.

Nobody is high on all eight, and the profiles that come closest tend to belong
to people who are pleasant, competent, and oddly difficult to recommend for
anything in particular. Being unmistakably one thing requires not being several
others. The distribution is the signal, not the total.

This has a practical edge. When you find yourself repeatedly failing at
something that sits in your bottom two, the useful response is usually to build
a working relationship with someone whose top two include it — not to spend
three years dragging yourself to mediocre.

### How the eight become one result

Each of the twelve shapes in the next chapter is a particular *mix* of these
eight — a direction rather than a score. Your answers make a mix too, and your
result is the shape whose mix points most nearly the same way as yours.

That last distinction matters more than it sounds. It means you're matched on
the **shape** of your profile, not the size of it. Someone who answers
decisively and someone who answers broadly get compared fairly, because it's
the proportions that are read, not the total. Enthusiasm doesn't move you up
the list.

If you're scoring on paper, Appendix B has the arithmetic. It's a page, and it
uses nothing worse than multiplication and a square root.
