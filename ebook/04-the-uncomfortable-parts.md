# Part Three — The uncomfortable parts

*Everything up to here has been about finding the thing. This part is about the
four ways it will let you down, and what each one is worth knowing.*

---

## 9. You versus the evidence

Score yourself on the self questions and the evidence questions separately —
Appendix B, Step 4 — and one of three things happens. Each means something
different and each has a different response.

### If they agree

What you think you're good at and what people come to you for are the same
thing.

This is rarer than it sounds and worth taking seriously as a finding. Most
people are quietly wrong about their own strongest suit, usually by one
position: they lead with the thing they had to work hardest for, because effort
made it feel like an achievement, while the thing they're actually sought out
for felt too easy to count.

If yours agree, the practical instruction is short and slightly boring: stop
auditing yourself and start spending it. You have been given permission you
didn't need but were apparently waiting for.

### If they're tilted

Same neighbourhood, different address. You lead with Insight; people come to
you for Signal. You think of yourself as Craft; the evidence says Care.

This is the most common result and the most immediately useful, because the
difference is worth money.

What's happening is usually this: the thing you value in yourself is the thing
you find *interesting*, and the thing people come to you for is the thing they
find *useful*. Those are related but not identical, and you've been leading
with the first in every conversation where the second would have landed better.

The fix is a rewrite, not a change of career. When you describe yourself —
profile, introduction, interview, the first thirty seconds of meeting someone
who might matter — lead with the second one. It's the half that's already been
validated by somebody other than you.

### If they're split

The two point in genuinely different directions. You rate yourself on one
thing; the demand you actually get is for another.

This is uncomfortable and it is the single most valuable output the method
produces, because it can only mean one of two things and both are actionable.

**Either you're undervaluing something.** It comes so easily you've never
counted it as a skill, so you don't mention it, don't charge for it, and don't
choose work that uses it — while other people keep turning up asking for
exactly that. This is the good version. The correction is free: start counting
it.

**Or you're in the wrong work.** The demand you're getting is real but it's
demand created by your situation rather than by you. A role can reliably pull
one particular thing out of a person for years, and if you answer the Evidence
questions from inside that role you'll faithfully describe what the job needs
rather than what you're for. This is the version worth acting on, and the
signal that it's happening is usually that the evidence side of your result
describes someone competent whom you don't especially want to be.

Neither of these resolves itself by waiting. That's the whole reason it's worth
measuring.

### How to disagree properly

You are allowed to think the result is wrong. But there is a right and a wrong
way to do it, and the difference is the difference between an instrument and a
horoscope.

**The wrong way** is to look at the twelve shapes, pick the one you'd rather
be, and adopt it. This feels like discernment and is actually just preference.
If you can override the output whenever you dislike it, the output was never
telling you anything.

**The right way** is to change the input. Go back to the Evidence questions —
they carry the most weight and they're the ones most often answered wrongly —
and answer them again, carefully, about a period when things were going well.
Then rescore. If the result moves, the first answers were the problem. If it
doesn't move, you have learned something you were resisting.

The online version has this built in: a "this doesn't sound like me" control
that shows you your runner-up in full and offers to re-run just the Evidence
questions. Use it. Arguing with the thing is part of using it.

## 10. Every superpower has a bill

Each of the twelve shapes comes with a cost. Not a caveat, not a "development
area" — a genuine bill that arrives whether or not you look at it.

This is not there to keep you humble. It's there for three concrete reasons.

**Because a description with no cost in it is a horoscope.** If your result
contained nothing you'd rather not hear, you would enjoy reading it and then
change nothing, because there'd be nothing to do. The bits that sting are the
bits with instructions attached.

**Because the cost is the strength.** These aren't separate. The Cartographer's
impatience *is* their pattern recognition, experienced by someone who has to
walk the map at normal speed. The Anchor's invisibility *is* their
reliability — you don't notice disasters that didn't happen. You cannot keep
the first and return the second; they're one thing seen from two sides.

**Because you will pay it anyway.** The choice isn't whether the bill arrives.
It's whether you see it coming.

### The general shape of the shadow

Read across all twelve and the same structure appears. Every shadow is the
strength applied where it doesn't belong, or applied past the point of
usefulness:

- The person who sees the whole picture gets impatient with people who need to
  walk it.
- The person who makes things gets attached to things that shouldn't have been
  made.
- The person who moves things forward moves them forward without asking where.
- The person who holds it all together holds too much and mentions none of it.
- The person who finds the third option finds one when the second was fine.
- The person who can explain anything can explain a bad idea beautifully.

Notice that in every case the failure mode is *more of the same thing*. This is
why "work on your weaknesses" is such poor advice for people who have already
found their strength. Your weakest signals are rarely what's costing you. What's
costing you is your best signal, running with nothing to stop it.

### What to actually do about it

Not fix it. You can't, and the attempt would cost you the strength.

Three things that work:

**Name it out loud, once, to the right person.** "I will over-build this if
nobody stops me." Said in advance, it converts a recurring irritation into a
shared joke and buys you an early warning system.

**Find the person whose top signal is your bottom one.** Not to become more
like them. To work with them. The most effective pairs are usually not two
similar people who agree; they're two people whose shadows don't overlap.

**Set one tripwire.** A single concrete condition that tells you the strength
has run past useful. *If I'm on the third redesign, stop. If I've asked more
than two more questions, decide. If I've said yes to a fourth thing this month,
say no.* A rule you set while calm is worth more than judgement you'll exercise
while absorbed.

## 11. What flattens you

Knowing what drains you is worth roughly as much as knowing what you're good
at, and almost nobody writes it down.

Note the distinction carefully, because it's easy to lose: this is not about
things you're bad at. It's about things that **cost you something to do, even
when you do them well**. Those are different lists and the second one is more
useful.

You can be excellent at chairing meetings and be flattened by them. You can be
the best explainer in the building and find repeating yourself for the fourth
time genuinely depleting. Competence tells you nothing about cost.

### The usual suspects

Long meetings that decide nothing. Being watched while you work. Repeating
yourself. Doing it someone else's way for no reason anyone can articulate.
Small talk with a lot of people. Waiting on other people to move. Detail work
whose purpose nobody will explain. Conflict you didn't choose.

Most people, reading that list, feel a spike of recognition at two or three.
Those are yours. And here's what makes them worth knowing: **they are usually
structural, not personal**. They're properties of a situation, which means
they can often be changed without changing you at all.

### The one that matters most

Beyond the list there's usually a single condition that doesn't merely drain
you but wastes you entirely — where your particular contribution becomes
impossible rather than just expensive:

- Somewhere nothing ever gets finished.
- Somewhere the answer's already been decided before you arrive.
- Somewhere nobody tells you what's really going on.
- Somewhere you never see who it's for.
- Somewhere there's never time to do it properly.

Pick yours. Then look honestly at where you currently spend your days.

If your answer describes your current situation, that is the most important
sentence in your result — and the correction is not to try harder. Effort
cannot fix a structural mismatch; it can only postpone the moment you notice.
People routinely spend years applying willpower to a problem that was actually
a question of setting, and conclude from the failure that something is wrong
with them.

### Saying it without sounding difficult

The obvious objection: you can't tell a new employer "I'm drained by meetings
that decide nothing." You'd sound like hard work.

But you can say the same thing forwards. Every drain has a positive form, and
the positive form is a perfectly normal thing to ask for in a conversation
about how you'd work:

- *Meetings that decide nothing* → "I'd rather have fewer, shorter meetings
  with a decision at the end."
- *Being watched while you work* → "I do my best work with a clear brief and
  some room."
- *Waiting on other people* → "I'm most useful where I can move without a lot
  of sign-off."
- *Detail work with no visible point* → "I work much better when I can see who
  it's for."

None of those sound difficult. They sound like someone who knows how they
work — which is the rarest thing in any interview.

## 12. The room you're good in

Here is a fact that gets lost in every conversation about talent: **the same
person is excellent in one setting and unremarkable in another, and the
difference is not effort.**

We know this perfectly well about other people. We say someone is a great
one-to-one manager but shouldn't run a town hall. We say someone's brilliant in
a crisis and bored the rest of the time. Then we turn to our own careers and
talk as though we carry a fixed quantity of ability that should perform
identically everywhere.

Four dimensions do most of the work.

### Company — alone ↔ in a room

Some people need real solitude to do the good part; some need one other person
to think against; some need a small team; some are only fully switched on with
an audience.

None of these is better. They're radically different requirements, and each is
routinely mistaken for a character flaw by people at the other end — the
solitary worker called antisocial, the audience-lover called a show-off.

### Brief — defined ↔ open

Some people are at their best given a clear specification, and will make it
excellent. Some want the goal fixed and the method theirs. Some want a rough
direction. Some are strongest facing a blank page — the situation where most
people freeze.

This one is worth knowing before you accept a job rather than after. "We're
still figuring it out" is either the most exciting sentence you'll hear that
week or a description of eighteen unhappy months.

### Tempo — calm ↔ pressure

Some people's quality collapses under urgency. Some sharpen. This is a
trade-off rather than a virtue: the person who is magnificent when it's on fire
is often mediocre when it isn't, and the person who needs calm to do their best
thinking will produce, in calm, work the crisis-performer cannot match.

Knowing which you are tells you what to say yes to.

### Horizon — short ↔ long

Some people need to finish something today. Some think in years, and short-cycle
work makes them feel that nothing they do matters.

Neither is more mature. But a long-horizon person in a job measured weekly will
look uncommitted, and a short-horizon person on a three-year programme will
look like they're losing interest — in both cases because the setting is
reading a mismatch as a character judgement.

### What to do with this

Write the four out as a sentence. It should read something like:

> I do my best work with **one good thinking partner**, **a rough direction**,
> **humane deadlines** and **season-long projects**.

That sentence is one of the most useful things you can carry into a job
interview, a project kick-off, or a conversation with your manager about how
the next six months should be arranged. It isn't a demand. It's the
specification for getting the best version of you, which is information the
other party actively wants and almost never gets offered.

And when a role is a bad fit on three of the four, no amount of enthusiasm will
compensate. Better to know before than to spend two years concluding you've
lost your edge.
