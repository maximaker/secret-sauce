# Part Four — Spending it

*An insight that stays on the page is worth nothing by Monday. This part is
about conversion.*

---

## 13. Your stage, your currency

The same finding is worth different things at different points in a life. Not
because the finding changes — because what you're trying to buy with it does.

One insight, four currencies.

### If you're still at school, or just out

Nobody expects you to have experience. That's not a weakness in your position;
it's the whole shape of it. It means experience cannot be what separates you
from the other applicants, because none of them have any either.

What separates you is a clear, specific account of how you think.

Most people your age write *I am hardworking and passionate*. This is
invisible. It is invisible in the specific sense that a reader's eye moves over
it without anything registering, because they have read it four hundred times
this month and it has never once predicted anything about anybody.

What you have instead is a particular way of being useful that you can point at
with real examples. That's the opposite of invisible.

**The move that matters most at this stage:** write down three times you did
this. Actual moments, with roughly a date attached. Not "I'm good at bringing
people together" — "in March, when the group project had stalled and two people
had stopped replying, I did *this specific thing*."

Vague strengths are worthless. Three concrete stories are unarguable, and
almost nobody your age brings them.

### If you're early in your working life

You are competing against people with the same qualification and the same job
title. Skills won't separate you: they're table stakes, everyone lists them,
and the list is the same list.

What separates you is a repeatable way of being useful that people remember
and, crucially, **refer**. Referral is the actual mechanism of an early career.
Most opportunities arrive because someone described you to someone else in one
sentence. You want to be in charge of that sentence.

**The move:** rewrite the first line of your profile so it says this instead of
your job title. Titles are interchangeable — there are forty thousand of
whatever you are. This isn't.

**The second move,** less comfortable: say yes this month to one piece of work
that needs exactly this, even if it's unglamorous. Reputation compounds off
repetition, not variety. Three of the same kind of thing makes you the person
people think of. Three different impressive things makes you someone who's been
busy.

### If you're well into it

By now you can do many things competently, and that is precisely the trap.
Competence pulls you toward being generally useful and away from being
specifically valuable, and the pull is strong because being generally useful is
rewarded in the short term every single time.

The question is no longer what you're able to do. It's what you should **stop**
doing so the thing below gets your best hours.

This is your signature: the work that only degrades when somebody else does it.
Everything else is a candidate for delegation, including things you're good at
and quietly enjoy.

**The move:** name one recurring thing on your plate that someone else could do
at eighty percent. Give it away this month. Eighty percent from them beats one
hundred percent from you if the price of your hundred is the thing only you can
do.

**The diagnostic:** look at your last six months. If less than a third of it
used this, that isn't a busy patch. That's a drift, and drifts don't self-
correct — they compound, because each month of not doing the thing makes you
marginally less obviously the person who does it.

### If you're deep in, or onto the next thing

The most valuable thing you carry now is not what you can produce yourself.
It's judgement that took decades to assemble and cannot be shortcut by anyone
younger, however capable they are.

The risk at this stage isn't running out of ability. It's letting the thing
stay implicit — so that it leaves the room when you do.

The task is to make it **transferable**: to name it, to put it where others can
watch it working, and to spend it where it compounds rather than where it's
merely wanted.

**The move:** pick one person who would be measurably better at their job if
they could do this. Give them one hour a month and nothing else. Not
mentorship in general — this specifically. That's how a capability survives the
person who has it.

**The second move:** choose where this is worth most now — advising,
governance, teaching, building one last thing — and decline something good to
protect it. At this stage the constraint is no longer capability. It's that
everything on offer is at least somewhat worth doing.

## 14. Say it where it counts

You now have something true about yourself. It is currently in a book, which is
the least useful place for it to be.

Four places it needs to exist as actual words. Write all four. It takes twenty
minutes and it's the difference between having done an exercise and having
changed something.

### The one-liner

One sentence, for where you only get one: a profile headline, a bio line, the
top of a CV, the answer to "what do you do?" at a party.

Constraints: under twelve words, first person, no job title, no adjectives
about yourself.

> *I make messy problems legible.*
> *I turn ideas into things that exist.*
> *I find the real problem, not the loud one.*

The test at the end of the process gives you one of these. If you're working on
paper, take your shape's line from the catalogue and cut it until it hurts.

### The spoken answer

Fifteen seconds, for "tell me about yourself" — the question everybody gets and
almost nobody has an answer to. Three beats:

1. **The claim.** What you're for, in one sentence.
2. **The mechanism.** The unusual combination that makes it work.
3. **The evidence.** What people come to you for.

> "I'm the person you want when the problem is a mess and nobody can agree on
> what it even is. I know the system well enough to see where it's wrong, and
> people come to me when something's quietly stopped working and nobody's said
> so."

Say it out loud four or five times, in your own words, until it stops sounding
like a script. It has to survive being spoken by someone slightly nervous.

### The written opener

Two sentences to build a paragraph on — a personal statement, a cover letter,
an About section, a promotion case.

1. *I'm the person who [the thing that's suspiciously easy].*
2. *People come to me when [your own sentence from Chapter 4].*
3. Then a clause that connects it to what you want, which is where the four
   stages differ: what a course should use, what a role should use, where your
   best hours should go, what you're most useful for now.

### What to ask for

The one people forget, and the one that changes the most.

> *I do my best work with [company], [brief], [tempo], [horizon]. What wastes
> me is [your top drain].*

This is not a list of demands. It's a specification for getting the best
version of you — information the other side actively wants and is almost never
offered. Use it in interviews, in project kick-offs, in the conversation about
how next quarter should run.

Most people negotiate salary and accept conditions. Conditions are usually
cheaper to change and make more difference to whether you're any good.

## 15. Four moves

Insight decays. It decays fast, and it decays completely — a realisation you
don't act on within about a week leaves nothing behind but a faint sense of
having once understood something about yourself.

So: four things, this week. Two from your shape, two from your stage. The
catalogue in Chapter 8 has the first pair; Chapter 13 has the second.

Rules that make them work:

**This week, not "soon".** Every move in this book is deliberately small enough
to do in an hour. If you find yourself scheduling one for next month, you've
mentally reclassified it as a project, and projects about yourself don't
happen.

**Concrete or it doesn't count.** "Reflect on my strengths" is not a move.
"Send this sentence to Sam and ask if it sounds like me" is a move. The test is
whether someone else could tell you'd done it.

**Two of the four should involve another person.** Anything you can complete
entirely inside your own head will be quietly downgraded, because nothing
external changes when you skip it.

**Expect one to be uncomfortable.** Usually the one that involves saying the
thing out loud to someone whose opinion you care about. That's the one with the
most in it.

### The most common failure

People read the result, recognise themselves, feel a genuine and pleasant sense
of having understood something — and do nothing, because the recognition itself
was satisfying enough.

This is the horoscope failure arriving by the back door. It doesn't require the
description to be vague; it only requires that reading it feels like enough.

The defence is mechanical rather than motivational: pick one move, do it before
you close the book, and let the other three follow or not.

## 16. Read this again in six months

Your result is not a fixed property of you, and treating it as one is the last
mistake available.

It describes how you are answering *now*, which is shaped by what you've been
doing for the last year or two. That's not a flaw in the method — that
sensitivity is what makes it useful. But it means the result has a shelf life,
and the honest number is somewhere between six and eighteen months.

### What to look for on a re-read

**If it's the same:** good. You have stability, and you now have two data points
that agree, which is worth considerably more than one. Spend it harder.

**If the evidence side has moved:** the most common change, and usually caused
by a change in situation rather than in you. New job, new team, new
responsibilities — people start coming to you for different things. Worth
asking whether the new demand suits you better or worse than the old one.

**If the self side has moved:** rarer and more interesting. Something has
changed in what you value about yourself. Sometimes this is growth. Sometimes
it's a role slowly convincing you that its priorities are yours.

**If the gap has widened:** pay attention. A self/evidence gap that grows over
a year is one of the earliest reliable signals of a bad fit, and it shows up in
this measurement well before it shows up as unhappiness.

### The thing that doesn't change

Underneath the movement, most people find something that stays.

Not the archetype, necessarily — those sit close together and a few points can
move you between neighbours. But the top signal, and the particular flavour of
what's suspiciously easy: those tend to hold across years, jobs, and quite
substantial changes of circumstance.

That's the actual answer to the question this book set out to ask. Not a label.
The thing you keep doing, in every setting, whether or not it's rewarded,
whether or not you've noticed.

You've probably been doing it all along. The only real change available is
doing it on purpose.
