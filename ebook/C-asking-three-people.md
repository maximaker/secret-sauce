# Appendix C — Asking three other people

Everything in this book except the Evidence questions relies on your own
account of yourself. The Evidence questions try to correct for that by asking
you to recall what other people do — but it's still you doing the recalling,
and you're still the least reliable witness available.

There's a version that fixes this properly, and it costs you one slightly
awkward message.

## Why it's worth the awkwardness

The Reflected Best Self work at Michigan found something consistent: when
people collect short accounts from others about when they'd seen them at their
best, the same capability gets named repeatedly by people who have never met
each other — and it's frequently not the thing the subject would have led with.

Other people have information you structurally cannot have. They've watched you
do the effortless thing and found it remarkable, which you didn't, because it
felt like nothing. And they have *chosen* to come to you rather than to
somebody else, which is much stronger evidence than an opinion.

## Who to ask

Three people, and the composition matters more than the number:

- **One from work** — someone who has seen you under pressure.
- **One from outside work** — a friend, a sibling, someone from a club or a
  team. They see a version of you that isn't performing a role.
- **One who has known you a long time** — ideally from before you had a
  professional identity to protect.

Deliberately avoid three people from the same context. Three colleagues will
describe your job.

## What to send

Keep it short and make it easy to answer badly, which is the only way you'll
get an answer at all.

> Hi — doing a slightly self-indulgent exercise and you're one of three people
> I'd trust to answer honestly. Four questions, two minutes, no wrong answers:
>
> 1. What do you actually come to me for? Not what I'm good at in general —
>    what you'd specifically ask *me* rather than someone else.
> 2. Think of a time you saw me at my best. What was I doing?
> 3. What do I do that I probably don't realise is unusual?
> 4. What's the thing about me that's useful right up until it isn't?
>
> Blunt is better than kind. Thank you.

That fourth question is the one that earns the exercise. It gives explicit
permission to name the shadow, which people will otherwise carefully avoid, and
it's usually the answer you'll still be thinking about a week later.

## What to do with the answers

**Don't read them one at a time and react.** Collect all three, then read them
together in one sitting. The signal is in the repetition, not in any single
reply, and reading them individually turns the exercise into three small
emotional events instead of one piece of evidence.

Then:

1. **Underline every repeated word or idea.** If two of three independently say
   something like *you see what's coming*, that's your top signal, and it
   outranks anything you'd have said about yourself.
2. **Re-answer the Evidence questions using their words, not yours.** This is
   the whole point. Go back to V1, V2 and V4 in Appendix A and answer as if
   your three respondents were filling them in.
3. **Rescore.** Compare the result to the one you got alone.

## Reading the difference

**If it barely moves:** your self-knowledge is unusually good. Trust the
result.

**If it moves a lot:** the version you got on your own was your self-image, and
this one is closer to what's actually happening. Take the second one.

The gap between the two is a direct measurement of how well you know yourself
on this particular question. Most people find it wider than they expected,
which is uncomfortable for about an hour and useful for years.

## One warning

Ask people who like you and will tell you the truth. Those two conditions are
both necessary and they are not the same condition.

Someone who likes you and won't be honest will give you a warm, useless
paragraph. Someone honest who doesn't much like you will give you a sharp
answer to a question you didn't ask. You want the narrow overlap: people
invested enough to be careful and secure enough to be blunt.

You already know who those three people are. That you thought of them
immediately is itself worth noticing.
