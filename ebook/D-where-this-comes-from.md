# Appendix D — Where this comes from

A short, honest account of what this method is built on, what's borrowed, what's
invented, and what you should not take on trust.

## What's borrowed

**The Reflected Best Self Exercise** (Center for Positive Organizations,
University of Michigan). The idea that the highest-quality information about
your strengths comes from other people's accounts of you, gathered from
different parts of your life and read for what repeats. The Evidence questions
and the whole self-versus-evidence comparison come from here. Appendix C is
essentially a compressed version of it.

**Forced-choice item design.** Making you choose between options rather than
rate each one is standard practice for reducing socially desirable responding.
The nuance, which is easy to get wrong: recent work finds that forced-choice
and Likert items recover much the same information *including* social
desirability, and that what actually reduces the bias is making the options
equally attractive. That's why no question here has an obviously admirable
answer — the format alone wouldn't have achieved anything.

**Short-scale reliability.** The rule of thumb that three items per construct
clears the usual reliability threshold and five is better. This is why the test
has as many questions as it does. An earlier version had eleven, which gave
each of the eight signals only two to four actual data points — thin enough
that the result rested on very little.

**Cosine similarity.** Standard vector mathematics, used here so that profiles
are matched on shape rather than magnitude — meaning a decisive answerer and a
broad answerer are compared fairly, and enthusiasm doesn't move you up the
list.

## What's invented

The eight signals, the twelve shapes, the four E's framework, the condition
axes, and all of the copy. These are a designed vocabulary, not a validated
taxonomy. They were built to be recognisable and useful rather than derived
from factor analysis, and you should hold them accordingly.

## What you should not take on trust

**The twelve shapes are not established categories.** They're a useful carving
of a continuous space. A slightly different set of twelve would work about as
well. If you find yourself between two, that's the space showing through the
carving, not a defect in you.

**The frequency percentages describe the instrument, not humanity.** When the
result says a shape turns up in a few percent of profiles, that is measured by
answering the questions at random a few hundred thousand times and counting.
It tells you how distinctive your combination is *within the space these
questions can produce*. It is not a claim about how many people are like you,
because that would require collecting real answers, which the test deliberately
doesn't do. This distinction is easy to blur and worth refusing to blur.

**No predictive validity has been established.** Nobody has run this against
job performance, satisfaction, or income over time. It has face validity — it
tends to describe people in ways they and their friends recognise — and that is
a genuinely lower standard.

**It measures how you're answering now.** Which is shaped by the last year or
two of your life. Chapter 16 is about that, and it's why the honest shelf life
is six to eighteen months rather than forever.

## What it is for

Given all of the above, a fair description of what this is:

A structured way of asking yourself four questions you would otherwise not ask
in an organised fashion, with a vocabulary for the answers, an arithmetic that
stops you from simply picking the flattering option, and a set of words you can
use afterwards.

That's less than a psychometric instrument and considerably more than a quiz.
It's most useful to people who have a vague sense of what they're for and no
language for it — which, in our experience of watching people take it, is
almost everyone.

## Colophon

The test is at
[secret-sauce-eta.vercel.app](https://secret-sauce-eta.vercel.app). It's free,
requires no account, and nothing you answer is transmitted anywhere — the
scoring runs entirely in your browser, which is also why it can't tell you how
you compare to other people.

The catalogue in Chapter 8 and Appendices A and B are generated directly from
the test's own source, so the book cannot drift out of step with the thing it
documents.
